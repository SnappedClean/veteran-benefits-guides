Agent Orange VA Claims: Presumptive Conditions, Exposure Rules and Disability Compensation

For many Vietnam-era veterans, the most important part of an Agent Orange claim is understanding VA's presumptive rules. If qualifying service and a listed condition are established, VA can presume the connection instead of requiring the veteran to prove the herbicide caused the illness in an individual case.

Quick answer: An Agent Orange presumptive claim generally turns on two major questions: did the veteran have qualifying service that VA recognizes for presumed herbicide exposure, and has the veteran been diagnosed with a condition on VA's Agent Orange presumptive list? The PACT Act expanded both the condition list and qualifying locations.

What Is an Agent Orange Presumptive VA Claim?

Ordinarily, a VA disability claim requires evidence of a current disability, an in-service event, injury, or disease, and a connection between the two. A presumption can remove part of that evidentiary burden. For Agent Orange, VA presumes certain veterans were exposed to herbicides because of where and when they served. If that veteran later develops a listed presumptive condition, the veteran generally does not need to prove the specific exposure event or obtain the same type of individual medical nexus evidence that a non-presumptive claim may require.

Presumptive does not mean automatic payment. VA still needs to verify qualifying service, confirm the diagnosis, determine the severity under the rating schedule, and establish other claim details such as the effective date.

Where Does VA Presume Agent Orange Exposure?

Vietnam service is the best-known category, but the recognized locations are broader today. The PACT Act and related changes expanded presumptive exposure to additional places and time periods. VA's current guidance includes qualifying service in Vietnam and specified service in Thailand, Laos, Cambodia, Guam, American Samoa, and other covered areas or waters during defined periods.

Because the date windows and geographic rules can be precise, use the official VA exposure page to compare your service dates and location. Veterans who do not meet a presumptive-location rule can still present evidence of actual herbicide exposure when the facts support it.

What Conditions Are Presumptive for Agent Orange?

VA's Agent Orange list includes numerous cancers and non-cancer illnesses. Examples include prostate cancer, respiratory cancers including lung cancer, bladder cancer, multiple myeloma, non-Hodgkin's lymphoma, chronic B-cell leukemias, Type 2 diabetes, ischemic heart disease, Parkinson's disease and Parkinsonism, peripheral neuropathy under specified rules, hypothyroidism, hypertension, and monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance (MGUS).

The list can be updated by law or regulation, so veterans should check VA's current page rather than relying on an old printed list. The PACT Act specifically added hypertension and MGUS to the Agent Orange presumptive-condition list.

How the PACT Act Changed Agent Orange Claims

The PACT Act expanded toxic-exposure benefits across several generations of veterans. For Agent Orange claims, two particularly important changes were adding hypertension and MGUS as presumptive illnesses and adding new presumptive geographic locations and date ranges.

This means some veterans who were denied years ago may now have a different path because the law changed. A prior denial is not necessarily the end of the story when a new presumption is created. Review the old decision, compare it with the current law, and use VA's current claim or Supplemental Claim process as appropriate.

How to File an Agent Orange Disability Claim

  1. Identify the diagnosed condition. Use actual medical records, not only symptoms.
  2. Document qualifying military service. Service personnel records, DD214 information, unit records, orders, or other records may establish dates and locations.
  3. Check VA's current presumptive exposure rules. Match your service to the official location and time period.
  4. Check the current presumptive-condition list. Do not rely on an old internet article.
  5. File the VA disability claim. Clearly identify the medical condition being claimed.
  6. Submit relevant private records or authorize VA to obtain them.
  7. Attend any C&P exam VA schedules. Presumptive service connection does not eliminate the need to evaluate the severity of the disability.

What Evidence Can Help an Agent Orange Claim?

Useful evidence can include a current diagnosis, pathology reports, cardiology records, oncology records, diabetes treatment records, medication lists, VA treatment records, private medical records, and military documents showing qualifying service. If the claim depends on actual exposure rather than a presumption, statements and records describing the location and circumstances of exposure may become more important.

For a survivor claim or a claim involving a deceased veteran, the medical and service records can also matter in determining whether a service-connected condition contributed to death and whether survivor benefits may be available.

What If Your Condition Is Not on the Presumptive List?

A condition not appearing on the Agent Orange list is not automatically barred from service connection. Presumptive rules are an easier evidentiary route for listed conditions; they do not prevent a veteran from attempting to prove direct service connection. A direct claim generally requires competent evidence connecting the current disability to military service or exposure.

That distinction is important because internet discussions sometimes treat the presumptive list as the only list of conditions that can ever be connected to herbicide exposure. It is not.

What If an Agent Orange Claim Was Denied?

Read the exact reasons in the VA decision letter. A denial might involve lack of a current diagnosis, service-location verification, an effective-date issue, or a condition that was not presumptive under the law at the time. Modern decision-review options include a Supplemental Claim with new and relevant evidence, Higher-Level Review when you believe VA made an error using the existing record, or an appeal to the Board of Veterans' Appeals.

When the law changes and creates a new presumption, the appropriate path can depend on the history of the specific claim. An accredited Veterans Service Organization representative, accredited claims agent, or accredited attorney can help with complex prior-denial and effective-date questions.

Avoid claim mills: VA accreditation matters. Be cautious with businesses that promise guaranteed ratings, charge questionable fees, or tell you to hide evidence. VA provides a searchable accreditation system for recognized representatives.

Agent Orange VA Claim FAQ

Do I need a nexus letter for an Agent Orange presumptive condition?

A qualifying presumption generally supplies the connection that a traditional direct-service-connection claim would otherwise need to establish, although VA still needs evidence of the diagnosis and qualifying service. Individual cases can involve additional issues.

Is hypertension an Agent Orange presumptive condition?

Yes. VA added hypertension as an Agent Orange presumptive condition under the PACT Act.

What if I served in Thailand or Guam?

PACT Act-related rules expanded presumptive herbicide-exposure locations and periods. Check VA's current official location list against your exact dates and duty location.

Can I reopen an old Agent Orange denial?

A prior denial may be reviewable, particularly when the law or presumptive list has changed or when new and relevant evidence is available. The correct review path depends on the claim history.
